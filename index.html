How I Think

These aren't slogans. Each one is a belief I formed by being wrong first. Click any to see where it came from.

01

Governance before autonomy.

Agents without guardrails are just expensive bugs.

The Belief

Every agentic system built in regulated environments teaches the same lesson: autonomy is earned, not assumed. The instinct is to give the LLM as much latitude as possible. The reality is that in banking, insurance, and compliance contexts, one silent failure in production costs more than all the time saved automating. Governance and determinism go in first; autonomy unlocks incrementally as trust is established.

Where This Came From

Building the Nimbus agentic orchestration layer for banks: KYC refresh, reconciliation, credit ops. The biggest architectural debate was "how much should the agent decide alone?" Pilots with broad tool access revealed failure modes that were silent, hard to trace, and catastrophic for audit. The shift to a strict phase model (Pre-LLM Guardrails → LLM → Post-LLM Guardrails → Tool → HITL → Verification) felt bureaucratic on paper. In production, it was the only thing that made the system trustworthy enough for enterprise sign-off.

"In regulated systems, the agent's job is to make the human's decision better, not to replace it."
02

Confidence scoring is not optional. It's the product.

Automation without a fallback is just liability at scale.

The Belief

Every LLM-powered extraction or classification pipeline has a failure rate. The question isn't whether it fails. It's whether the system knows when it's failing and routes accordingly. Confidence scoring and structured exception handling aren't engineering nice-to-haves. They're what separates a liability from a product.

Where This Came From

Processing 50K+ KYC/AML documents monthly at Schbang: identity proofs, financial statements, variable-quality scans. Pure OCR failed on unstructured content. Pure LLM extraction hallucinated on structured tables. The hybrid pipeline worked only when field-level confidence scoring with exception routing was added. Every document below threshold went to a human queue. The 70% reduction in manual ops came not from removing humans. It came from sending humans only the cases that actually needed them.

"Confidence scoring is what turns automation from a cost-saver into a decision system."
03

The best AI product is the one users forget is AI.

When AI is done right, it disappears into the experience.

The Belief

The "AI-powered" label is a liability if the experience underneath it doesn't work. Users in high-stakes situations (filing an insurance claim, submitting a KYC document, doing their onboarding) don't care about the model. They care about whether they got the right answer, whether it was fast, and whether they understood it. The moment they feel like they're interacting with a system rather than getting a job done, trust is broken.

Where This Came From

Designing the GROMO AI agent for the B2C insurance app. The temptation was to make the AI identity prominent: chatbot window, "AI says..." headers. The research told a different story. Users in Tier-2/3 cities wanted to renew their policy, find a hospital, start a claim. The design decision was to make AI the engine of every feature (RAG-backed policy cards, OCR ingestion, consent-scoped desk links) and keep the surface layer simple, fast, and jargon-free. The AI worked best when you couldn't see it.

"If the user notices the AI, you probably haven't finished the product."
04

RAG without citation is just confident hallucination.

In high-stakes domains, users need to see the source, not just the answer.

The Belief

Retrieval-augmented generation solves the hallucination problem only if you complete the loop: show users which clause, which document, which sentence the answer came from. Without citation, RAG is just a hallucination with better vocabulary. In domains where the answer has financial or legal consequences (insurance exclusions, credit decisions, compliance flags) unexplained confidence is actively dangerous.

Where This Came From

Building explainable policy cards for the insurance app. The brief was "help users understand their coverage." The easy version: ask the LLM, surface the answer. The right version: clause-grounded RAG with citation logic, confidence chips, and a "why?" path for every answer. Users in testing challenged answers they couldn't verify, and trusted answers they could trace. The citation wasn't a legal feature. It was the trust feature.

"Explainability isn't a compliance checkbox. It's the interface between AI confidence and user trust."
05

Audit trails are the product, not the plumbing.

In regulated environments, traceability is the feature that closes deals.

The Belief

Every agentic system built for enterprise needs to answer one question from risk/compliance teams before go-live: "what happened, when, and why?" Teams that treat logging as an engineering afterthought discover, usually at the worst moment, that they can't answer it. Auditability designed in from day one, because it's not just compliance overhead. It's what gets the system trusted.

Where This Came From

The lead enrichment HITL workflow: n8n + GPT-5 + Claude reviewing 40–80K leads before outreach. The original design sent emails directly after AI review. Stakeholder concern: "what if something goes wrong at scale?" The solution was deceptively simple: write to Gmail drafts, not send. Every draft was auditable, correctable, traceable. That one decision (drafts, not sends) was the trust-building mechanism that unlocked stakeholder buy-in for the entire automation.

"If you can't explain what the system did and why, you don't have a product. You have a black box."
06

The best technical decision and the best organizational decision are rarely the same.

Your job is to find the path that produces the best outcome, not the cleanest architecture.

The Belief

Product managers in AI-heavy roles are often the only person in the room who sees both the technical constraints and the organizational dynamics. The trap is optimizing purely for architecture quality and losing the political capital needed to actually ship. The skill is finding the path that produces the best real-world outcome, not the one that would win a design review.

Where This Came From

Multiple enterprise sales cycles at Solytics: presenting AI architectures to bank CISOs, CROs, and CTOs who had already formed strong views before the meeting. The cleanest architecture designed was rejected not because it was wrong, but because it required too much change too fast. The version that shipped was 80% as elegant, phased differently, with more human controls in the early stages. It ran in production. The clean version never did.

"A shipped 80% solution beats an undeployed 100% one every time."

The $40M Decision Your CTO Has Already Made

Build vs. BuyStakeholder Politics

It's Q3 planning. Your CTO has informally committed to a major vendor for an AI platform worth $40M over 3 years. Your user research shows the vendor's product doesn't map to how your 50K employees actually work. Engineering says they could build 80% of the functionality in 9 months. The CTO has a personal relationship with the vendor's CEO. You have two weeks to present your recommendation.

The Options

A

Endorse the vendor. Protect the relationship, find workarounds for the gaps.

B

Present a hybrid: buy the core, build the last mile. Reframe it as "maximizing the investment."

Chosen
C

Make the full case for build internally, with data, and accept the political risk.

Chose Option B

Option C is the principled move but burns political capital needed for the next 10 decisions. Option A is abdication. The hybrid reframe lets the CTO save face, gets better user outcomes, and creates a forcing function: if the vendor can't meet the integration spec in 6 months, the data justifies pivoting. Playing the long game.

The Tradeoff

Short-term completeness traded for organizational trust. The hybrid isn't the ideal product architecture. It's the best product outcome achievable inside a real organization.

"At VP level, the best technical decision and the best organizational decision are rarely the same. Your job is to find the path that produces the best outcome, not the cleanest recommendation."

Ship the Agent or Add Another Guardrail?

Agentic SystemsLaunch Decisions
Agentic SystemsLaunch Decisions

The Situation

Three weeks from launch of an agentic KYC workflow at a major bank. The agent handles 2,000 document reviews per day. In staging: 94% extraction accuracy. Strong, but a 6% error rate means ~120 wrong extractions daily. Engineering says adding another guardrail layer pushes the timeline by 6 weeks. The business needs to show the AI investment is working. Compliance hasn't formally signed off.

The Options

A

Ship with 94% accuracy. Add monitoring and fix in prod.

B

Add the guardrail layer. Push 6 weeks. Brief the business on why.

C

Ship with 94% accuracy but route all low-confidence cases to human review, reducing automation from 100% to ~70%.

Chosen
Chose Option C

Option A is "move fast and hope." In a regulated environment, a silent error at scale is a compliance event, not a product bug. Option B is the right architecture but kills business momentum at the worst time. Option C is the honest answer: ship a system that works well enough, as long as the limits are explicit. The 70% automation rate is still transformational. The 30% human review is a feature. It's the confidence boundary made visible.

The Tradeoff

The headline number (100% automation) traded for a system compliance could actually sign off. The 70% became the story used to build trust. Six months later, with training data from the human review queue, 97% was reached and the queue was removed.

"The confidence boundary is not a weakness to hide. It's the honest edge of your system, and the most important thing to make visible."

The Feature Users Asked For vs. The One They Actually Need

User ResearchPrioritization
User ResearchPrioritization

The Situation

B2C insurance app. Unanimous user feedback: they want a price comparison feature. #1 request for two quarters. Analytics show 67% of churned users left during the renewal flow, not purchase. Exit surveys: "I didn't understand what I was renewing." The comparison feature: 10 weeks. Fixing the renewal flow (explainable policy summaries, document status chips, one-tap autopay): 4 weeks.

The Options

A

Build the comparison feature. Users asked for it. Deliver what they want.

B

Fix the renewal flow. Address what the data says is actually causing churn.

Chosen
C

Do both: a lighter comparison (link-out) + the renewal flow fix.

Chose Option B

Users asked for comparison because they were trying to solve a trust problem, not a price problem. "Am I getting a good deal?" is the surface question. The real one is "do I understand what I have?" Comparison gives the illusion of control. Explainable policy summaries give actual understanding. The churn data was unambiguous: 67% left at renewal. Building the comparison feature would have been building the wrong thing faster.

The Tradeoff

The most-requested feature deferred for a full quarter. The conversation with stakeholders was uncomfortable. The renewal fix shipped in 4 weeks, churn dropped, and the comparison feature went on the roadmap with better-defined requirements from what we learned.

"Users tell you what they want. Data tells you what they need. The job is to know the difference, and explain why you chose one over the other."
